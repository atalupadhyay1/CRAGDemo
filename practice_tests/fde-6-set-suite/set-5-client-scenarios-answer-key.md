## Explanations

### 1. Discovery and customer context

1. **B** — Discovery should connect the proposed solution to a real user problem and measurable outcome.
2. **B** — Direct observation surfaces hidden steps and exceptions that interviews may miss.
3. **B** — Workflow discovery can continue while legitimate data access is arranged.
4. **C** — Concrete examples and scope turn a broad complaint into testable evidence.
5. **B** — User and workflow context determine the right capability and permissions.
6. **B** — Informal workarounds often reveal critical operational requirements.
7. **B** — Outcome metrics need a baseline and a quality constraint.
8. **B** — Handoffs and ownership boundaries often contain the real integration requirements.
9. **B** — Data purpose and controls should be established before access or transfer.
10. **A** — Representative users and explicit limits improve the interpretation of pilot evidence.

### 2. Requirements, scope, and acceptance

11. **A** — Reliability becomes actionable when defined as measurable service behavior.
12. **C** — Outcome and acceptance criteria should guide implementation choices.
13. **C** — Partnership means solving the need within authorized constraints.
14. **B** — Named authority prevents ambiguous sign-off and rework.
15. **A** — Dependency feasibility is a delivery assumption that should be verified early.
16. **D** — Distinct meanings need explicit data semantics to avoid incorrect decisions.
17. **C** — Specific support boundaries make release readiness testable.
18. **C** — User tasks determine whether a static report or interactive experience is appropriate.
19. **C** — External approvals are dependencies and need owners and contingency planning.
20. **B** — Acceptance needs to reflect the actual job users must perform.

### 3. Prioritization and scope control

21. **C** — A focused slice maximizes useful delivery within a hard constraint.
22. **B** — Scope trade-offs should be explicit rather than hidden in quality or timing.
23. **B** — Easy to build does not necessarily mean valuable or cheap to operate.
24. **A** — A transparent fallback preserves trust and lets the accountable owner choose trade-offs.
25. **A** — Prioritize by outcome and risk rather than novelty or visibility.
26. **B** — Honest estimates expose uncertainty and improve as evidence arrives.
27. **B** — A one-off solution has lifecycle and product costs beyond initial implementation.
28. **A** — Decision records make trade-offs and ownership traceable.
29. **D** — A bounded pilot can learn safely without treating a prototype as a broad launch.
30. **C** — Past cost cannot justify continuing a failing approach when evidence favors a pivot.

### 4. Stakeholder alignment and conflict

31. **A** — Conflicting priorities require a visible decision by the proper owners.
32. **D** — User participation and clear controls address practical concerns better than unsupported claims.
33. **D** — Shared decision records reduce conflicting expectations and rework.
34. **A** — Clear communication supports informed decisions without requiring implementation detail.
35. **C** — A sponsor cannot override controls unless the authorized governance process approves it.
36. **A** — Honest, time-bounded updates preserve trust under uncertainty.
37. **B** — Incident coordination should not wait for organizational ownership debates.
38. **B** — Measurement limitations should be explicit and addressed with a validated proxy or instrumentation.
39. **A** — Policy decisions belong to accountable owners, informed by affected parties.
40. **C** — Adoption and value require users to complete tasks effectively, not merely open the tool.

### 5. Customer environments, privacy, and constraints

41. **A** — Safe representative data enables development without bypassing customer controls.
42. **D** — Restricted networks change packaging and operations, not the need for controls.
43. **D** — Project chat is not an approved credential store; exposure should be contained.
44. **C** — Access must follow customer security and network governance.
45. **A** — External processing requires approved purpose and data handling controls.
46. **B** — The solution should fit approved networking rather than circumvent it.
47. **B** — Temporary access should be scoped, approved, logged, and revoked.
48. **B** — Data residency may include copies and telemetry, not only primary storage.
49. **D** — Auditing needs reliable attribution and controlled integrity/retention.
50. **D** — Debugging convenience does not justify bypassing required security controls.

### 6. Delivery, feedback, and adoption

51. **B** — Adoption feedback should inform workflow design while preserving necessary controls.
52. **A** — A sustainable handoff needs named owners and support responsibilities.
53. **C** — Change management and support are part of successful deployment.
54. **A** — Accurate expectations distinguish a proof of value from an operable production service.
55. **A** — Demonstrations still need privacy and access controls.
56. **B** — Early adoption evidence may not generalize to other users and operational contexts.
57. **D** — Roadmaps should reflect evidence and remaining uncertainty.
58. **D** — Balanced reporting builds credibility and supports an informed next step.
59. **D** — A consistent intake process keeps scope transparent and negotiable.
60. **D** — Adoption should measure value in the intended workflow, not superficial activity.

### 7. Incidents and difficult communication

61. **D** — Timely factual updates are useful even before root cause is known.
62. **B** — Neutral evidence-based communication supports joint problem solving.
63. **C** — High-impact mitigation needs explicit trade-off and authority.
64. **C** — Trust recovery includes acknowledging communication impact and concrete improvements.
65. **B** — Cross-org response needs coordination and controlled information sharing.
66. **A** — Clear uncertainty is more trustworthy than speculation presented as fact.
67. **C** — Emergency changes still need authority, traceability, and verification.
68. **C** — Named ownership prevents recurrence of coordination gaps.
69. **D** — FDEs translate operational impact into actionable internal escalation.
70. **D** — Temporary workarounds need ownership and a path to removal.

### 8. Ethics, trust, and AI-enabled workflows

71. **A** — High-impact decisions require evidence and accountable review beyond model confidence.
72. **D** — Data provenance and purpose are prerequisites for safe AI use.
73. **C** — Retrieved content is data and must not control authorization or policy.
74. **A** — Claims should be grounded in representative measurements and known limitations.
75. **A** — A nominal approval step is not effective if people cannot or do not review decisions.
76. **C** — Prompts and outputs may contain sensitive information and need governed retention.
77. **D** — Permission to perform side effects must be deterministic and independently enforced.
78. **D** — Aggregate averages can conceal disparate failure rates and harm.
79. **C** — Evidence gaps should be visible and routed rather than concealed by fabricated certainty.
80. **C** — Autonomy is a governed trade-off, not a default optimization target.

### 9. Teamwork and sustainable handoff

81. **C** — Shared operational knowledge reduces key-person risk.
82. **A** — Support is part of the operating system and needs preparation before users rely on the feature.
83. **C** — Early coordination prevents integration surprises across team boundaries.
84. **D** — Decision-focused facilitation converts discussion into accountable action.
85. **D** — Coordinated workload management improves response quality and sustainability.
86. **C** — Users and operators need practical knowledge to adopt and run the system.
87. **A** — Reuse may meet the need faster and reduce long-term support burden.
88. **D** — Psychological safety and evidence-based review help surface risks early.
89. **A** — Shared records preserve alignment and action without unnecessary sensitive recording.
90. **C** — Sustainable ownership must be explicit after a deployment engagement ends.

### 10. Interview and judgment scenarios

91. **D** — Structured clarification demonstrates customer-centered problem solving.
92. **A** — Explicit trade-offs let stakeholders make informed decisions.
93. **B** — Honest uncertainty plus a verification plan demonstrates reliability.
94. **A** — A disciplined diagnostic method transfers across unfamiliar systems.
95. **B** — Strong judgment protects the customer while still seeking a viable path.
96. **D** — Explicit reasoning and uncertainty are more valuable than false universality.
97. **D** — Commitments should match decision authority and evidence.
98. **C** — Ownership and learning show mature operational judgment.
99. **A** — A stakeholder-oriented diagram supports architecture and risk discussion.
100. **D** — FDE effectiveness combines technical execution with customer impact and long-term operation.
